# Restaurant pro forma: how to build one line by line

A restaurant pro forma is a projected income statement for a restaurant or café: expected sales, food and drink cost, labour, rent and other operating costs, period by period, ending in profit. Owners use it to test a concept, negotiate a lease, and as the starting point for a loan application. This guide walks through each line, gives a worked example and lists the checks to run before you show it to anyone.

If you'd rather have one built on your numbers, [StartupModel's café and restaurant model](/models/restaurant-coffee-shop-financial-projections) starts at $149.

## What goes into a restaurant pro forma

A useful pro forma has three layers.

1. **The drivers.** The few numbers that decide everything else: covers or customers per day, average spend, operating days, food cost percentage, rent, staffing.
2. **The calculation.** Revenue and costs derived from the drivers, with the same formula in every period.
3. **The output.** A profit line per month and per year, and ideally a downside and an upside version.

What a pro forma is **not**: a cash flow forecast. It shows profit, not cash. If you need to show a lender how a loan gets repaid, you also need a cash flow statement and a balance sheet. See [financial projections for a bank loan](/guides/financial-projections-for-bank-loan).

## Line by line

### Revenue

**Revenue = customers per day × average ticket × operating days.**

- *Customers per day.* Estimate full-traffic volume from comparable sites, a footfall count or your current trade, then add a ramp: opening traffic as a share of full, and months to get there.
- *Average ticket.* Not your menu price but what a typical customer spends: menu prices times a realistic mix.
- *Operating days.* Your opening schedule, net of planned closures.

Add a price increase per year and a modest traffic growth rate after the first year if you have a reason for them.

### Food and beverage cost

A percentage of revenue, built from your recipe costing and supplier quotes. Keep it as an input you can change, not a number buried in a formula.

### Labour

List roles, headcount and salary for each, and **add payroll taxes and benefits as a separate percentage**. Leaving these out is one of the most common reasons a pro forma looks better than reality. Add wage growth per year.

### Rent

The monthly figure from the lease or the landlord's offer. Rent is fixed while sales vary, so it is the line that hurts most in a slow year.

### Marketing, utilities and other

Marketing as a share of revenue or a monthly budget; utilities, insurance, card fees and the rest as monthly amounts.

### Below the line

EBITDA, then depreciation of the fit-out and equipment, interest on any loan, and tax. A pro forma that stops at EBITDA is fine for testing a concept but not for a lender.

## Worked example

Illustrative inputs only; these are not benchmarks.

| Input | Value |
|---|---|
| Customers per day at full traffic | 150 |
| Opening traffic | 60% of full |
| Months to full traffic | 6 |
| Average ticket | $6.40 |
| Operating days per month | 30 |
| Food & beverage cost | 32% of revenue |
| Rent | $4,800 per month |

A mature month: 150 × $6.40 × 30 = **$28,800** of revenue. Food and beverage cost at 32% is $9,216, leaving $19,584 of gross profit before rent, labour and everything else.

Month 1: 60% of 150 is 90 customers a day, so revenue is 90 × $6.40 × 30 = **$17,280**. Gross profit is $11,750 (rounded), and rent alone takes $4,800 of that. That gap between month 1 and a mature month is why the ramp has to be in the model.

## Add scenarios before anyone asks

A single pro forma answers the question "what if everything goes to plan?" Lenders, landlords and partners will ask the other question. Build three versions of the key drivers:

| Driver | Low | Base | High |
|---|---|---|---|
| Customers per day at full traffic | lower | your estimate | higher |
| Average ticket | lower | your estimate | higher |
| Food & beverage cost | higher | your estimate | lower |

Note that for cost drivers the low case means a **higher** cost. Our models check this ordering automatically on the Checks sheet, because a downside case with lower costs is a common and misleading mistake.

The best format is one switch that changes every sheet at once, rather than three copies of the file that drift apart.

## Checks before you share it

1. Every input in one place, labelled, with its source noted.
2. The same formula across every month: no manual overrides in month 7.
3. Annual totals equal the sum of the months.
4. Switching to the low case changes revenue and profit.
5. Margins within sensible bounds: gross margin never negative or above 100%.

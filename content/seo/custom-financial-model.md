## What "custom" should mean

Most people who search for a custom model have already tried something else. They opened a template and found it was built for another business. They asked a chatbot and got text rather than a working workbook. Or they got a quote from an analyst that was larger than the first year's marketing budget.

A model is custom when three things are true:

1. **The revenue build matches your business.** A café sells to customers per day at an average ticket. A software company sells subscriptions that renew and churn. A consulting firm sells billable hours. If the model forces your revenue into someone else's logic, every number that follows is wrong in a way nobody will notice until the bank asks.
2. **The inputs are yours.** Not a sample that you overwrite one cell at a time, hoping nothing breaks. Your opening traffic, your rent, your hiring plan, your loan.
3. **You can change it.** A model is a decision tool. If you can't change an assumption and watch the whole file follow, you have a report, not a model.

## How to judge any financial model in ten minutes

I have reviewed a lot of models over the years, for lenders, boards and founders. The checks below catch most of the problems, whoever built the file.

- **Click a revenue cell.** You should see a formula pointing at an assumption, not a typed number. Pasted values are the most common way a model goes stale.
- **Find the assumptions.** Each one should sit in its own labelled cell, in one place. If growth rates are buried inside formulas, nobody can review them.
- **Look across the columns.** The same formula should run across every period. A formula that changes in month 7 usually hides a manual fix.
- **Check the balance sheet.** Assets must equal liabilities plus equity in every period, not just at year end. If there is no balance sheet, ask how cash was calculated.
- **Change one assumption.** Halve sales growth. Does cash fall? Does the loan still get repaid? If nothing moves, the model is not wired.
- **Look for the downside.** A single forecast is a guess. A lender or investor will ask what happens if sales come in lower. A model with a scenario switch answers that in one click.

## The part most models skip: proof

Before a model is released, the numbers are recomputed independently in plain Python, outside the spreadsheet, for every scenario and every year. The workbook must match that recomputation within $1. If it doesn't, the file is not sent.

The Checks sheet travels with the file. Its formulas run again every time you change an input. They confirm, among other things, that:

- annual revenue and net income tie to the period detail;
- EBITDA reconciles to gross profit and operating costs;
- closing cash equals the sum of all net cash flows;
- the balance sheet balances in every period (Full);
- the scenario selector is valid and the low / base / high values are ordered the right way.

When you send the model to a banker, an investor or your accountant, they can open that sheet and see the result for themselves.

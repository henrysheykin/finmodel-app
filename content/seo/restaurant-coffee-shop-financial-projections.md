## The drivers that decide a café's numbers

Our café and restaurant template is built around the drivers an owner actually controls or can research. Each one has a low, base and high value.

| Driver | What to base it on |
|---|---|
| Customers per day at full traffic | Comparable sites, a footfall count at your location, your current sales if you already trade |
| Opening traffic, % of full | Be honest: few sites open at full traffic |
| Months to reach full traffic | The ramp-up lenders will ask about |
| Traffic growth per year after year 1 | Modest unless you have a reason |
| Average ticket | Your menu prices × a realistic basket |
| Price growth per year | Your planned price increases |
| Operating days per month | Your opening schedule |
| Food & beverage cost, % of revenue | Supplier quotes and your recipe costing |
| Rent per month | The lease or the landlord's offer |
| Marketing, % of revenue | Your launch and ongoing plan |
| Utilities & other per month | Quotes and comparable sites |
| Start-up capex (fit-out, equipment) | Contractor and supplier quotes |

On top of these sit the shared drivers every Full model has: headcount and salaries by role, payroll taxes and benefits, wage growth, receivable / inventory / payable days, depreciation, income tax, the owner's equity and the loan (amount, rate, term).

## How the revenue build works

The template calculates revenue as **customers per day × average ticket × operating days**, with a ramp from opening traffic to full traffic over the months you set.

An illustration, with made-up inputs (not benchmarks): full traffic of 150 customers a day, an average ticket of $6.40 and 30 operating days gives $28,800 of revenue in a mature month. If you open at 60% of full traffic and set 6 months to reach full traffic, month 1 revenue is $17,280, and the model steps up evenly until full traffic from month 7. Food and beverage cost, marketing and the rest follow from revenue or from your fixed monthly inputs.

The point of the ramp is credibility. A projection that shows a mature month on day one is the quickest way to lose a loan officer's trust.

## What a lender will look at in a restaurant projection

- **The ramp and the cash cushion.** Opening months usually lose money. The cash flow shows how deep the dip goes and whether the loan plus your equity covers it. If the low case runs out of cash in month 5, you want to find that before the bank does.
- **Food cost and labour.** These are the two lines a lender with restaurant experience will compare with what they see elsewhere. Put your recipe costing and your rota behind them.
- **Rent as a share of revenue.** Rent is fixed; revenue isn't. The scenario switch shows what the lease looks like in a slow year.
- **Debt service.** The model shows interest and principal repayments by month against the cash the business generates.
- **Uses of funds.** Fit-out and equipment appear as capital spending in the first period and are depreciated over time, so the P&L isn't distorted by a one-off cost.

The SBA's guidance for business plans asks for a five-year outlook with forecasted income statements, balance sheets and cash flow statements, monthly or quarterly in the first year ([SBA — Write your business plan](https://legacy.sba.gov/business-guide/plan-your-business/write-your-business-plan)). Your lender may want less or more; ask. The model supports 1, 3 or 5 years in monthly or annual detail.

## Common mistakes in café projections

1. **No ramp-up.** Covered above, and the most frequent.
2. **Average ticket from the menu, not the basket.** A $5 latte doesn't mean a $5 average ticket: food add-ons push it up, and cheaper drinks pull it down. Use the mix you expect.
3. **Staff costed at salary only.** Payroll taxes and benefits are a separate input in our models for a reason.
4. **Fit-out in the P&L.** Capital spending belongs in cash flow and the balance sheet, depreciated over time.
5. **No downside.** The low case is what the lender will think about anyway. Show it first.
